    The Vossberg heritage

    1884

    The origin

    Willem and Anton Vossberg were wheelwrights who could judge a spoke by the note it gave when struck. That discipline carried them from a farm on the edge of Haarlem into coachbuilding, and it has stayed with the marque ever since. Their carriages earned a reputation well beyond the Netherlands, and buyers came from across Europe to a small yard on the Spaarne. The same care went into the city's horse-drawn trams, proof that good work was never meant only for the few.

    1891

    The State Landau

    If Vossberg needed one moment to announce itself, this was it. The State Landau, built for a royal jubilee, was as ornate as anything the country had ever ordered, and it still leaves its coach house for the opening of parliament every autumn.

    1899

    The first Vossberg motorcar

    The brothers' next fascination was the combustion engine. Their first motorcar used a borrowed single-cylinder engine, but its body was shaped entirely by hand, and that was what people talked about. Vossberg never set out to build the fastest car. It set out to build the most honest one.

    1904

    A six-cylinder first

    Then came a car that changed the conversation: a six-cylinder racer with brakes on all four wheels, years before its rivals. Seen from today it reads like a footnote. At the time it was an act of nerve, a small Dutch firm deciding that the rules of the automobile did not apply to it.

    The sound of six cylinders

    Shown to crowds in Paris and Brussels, the engine came from a particular restlessness: more power for less effort, more speed with more calm. It is the same restlessness that drives the brand today.

    1906

    Beach sprints at Zandvoort

    By the time Vossberg lined up for the beach sprints at Zandvoort, its cars already wore a recognisable face: a softly arched radiator, gentler than anything else on the sand. It was a small thing, but it was theirs, and it would shape the marque for generations.

    1908

    Berlin to Rome

    Few trials asked as much of a car as the Berlin–Rome reliability run. A Vossberg 16 HP set out across the Alps on roads that were, in places, little more than mule tracks. There were no garages and no certainty, only the car and the question of whether it had been built well enough to arrive.

    Second place

    Five weeks later it arrived, second overall, having crossed a continent most makers would not have attempted. No trophy quite captured what that meant: a hand-built Dutch car tested against distance, weather and terrain, and proving itself entirely on its own terms.

    1911

    The sleeve-valve engine

    Vossberg's engineering rarely sat still. In 1911 a young engineer from Delft drew a quiet sleeve-valve four for the marque, a genuine departure that gave the cars a mechanical identity of their own.

    1913

    Grand touring saloons

    Around that engine came a new generation of touring saloons, their bodies increasingly enclosed as expectations of comfort changed. The instincts of the coachbuilder, warmth, comfort, a sense of occasion, sat easily beside modern engineering, because at Vossberg they had never been separate.

    1916

    Aviation

    During the war years the workshop turned its hands to aircraft, building wooden airframes for the country's first flying school. The family motto, Fortes Fortuna Adiuvat, was painted on every tail.

    1920

    The Aerolight

    Peace brought the Aerolight, a touring car with a body built like a fuselage: a light timber frame skinned in thin aluminium. It weighed a third less than its rivals and looked like nothing else on the road.

    1924

    Coast to coast

    An Aerolight crossed from Calais to Marseille in a single day and night, a record that stood for six years. Two years later the workshop fell silent, and the name slept for most of the century.

    1999

    The return

    Seventy-three years later, a group of enthusiasts bought the name, the drawings and a single surviving Aerolight. Their idea was simple: build cars the way the brothers would have, with today's engineering.

    2001

    V8 Corsa

    The first modern Vossberg was a mid-engined V8 coupé with an exposed gear linkage and a quilted cabin. It was raw, beautiful and not quite finished, which is exactly how its owners liked it.

    2003

    V8 Corsa S

    A longer, calmer road car followed, built for crossing Europe rather than lapping it, and the first Vossberg offered with a full set of fitted luggage.

    2008

    V12 Lusso

    The V12 Lusso brought twelve cylinders and a long bonnet back to the marque, along with bespoke luggage cut to fit its rear shelf.

    2015

    B-Concept

    A study for a four-seat grand tourer, the B-Concept tested ideas that would return years later in the V9 Aquila.

    2018

    V8 Aquila

    The first Aquila revived the lattice grille and the porthole tail lamps, and sold out before the first car was finished.

    2026

    V9 Aquila XXI

    Twenty-one cars, built one at a time in Haarlem. A new chapter that reads, unmistakably, like the first one.

    1884 — today

    Discover the V9 Aquila

    1904

    Vossberg racing history

    Vossberg's racing story falls into two chapters separated by most of a century. First came the six-cylinder racer and the reliability runs of the 1900s; then, after the marque's return, a modern programme that took the name to Sebring, Spa and Le Mans.

    The six-cylinder's debut

    Anton Vossberg had something to prove, and he built a car to prove it. Entered for the Paris–Bordeaux sprint, the six-cylinder racer arrived as the first of its kind, with four-wheel brakes and a top speed few believed. It retired with a broken spring, but not before leading for two hours.

    1908

    Entry in the Berlin–Rome run

    A standard production car entered privately, against a route that had never been driven before. Second overall was the result, and the beginning of a reputation for cars that simply did not stop.

    2002

    Return to motorsport

    Some comebacks are loud. Vossberg's began quietly at Sebring, where a single V8 Corsa started from the back of the grid and finished, which was all anyone had asked of it.

    Debut at Le Mans

    The Corsa reached Le Mans the following summer. A gearbox failure ended its race after fourteen hours, but the car had run in the top ten of its class until then.

    2005

    Twelve hours of Sebring

    Three years later the team returned to Sebring with two cars and finished fifth and eighth in class.

    2008

    Spa 24 Hours

    The final race of the programme: a class podium at Spa, in pouring rain, at four in the morning.
